Automated Genre Tagging from Song Lyrics: An NLP Pipeline¶

1. Problem Statement¶

Business context. I'm framing this around a music streaming platform's catalog team. Accurate genre metadata is what powers search, playlist curation, and recommendations behind the scenes, but in practice large catalogs — especially independent or user-uploaded tracks — often arrive with genre tags that are missing, inconsistent, or simply self-declared by the artist (plenty of people tag everything "Pop" because it's the most discoverable label, whatever the song actually sounds like). Re-tagging a catalog of this size by hand just isn't realistic.

Why it matters. Bad genre tags quietly wreck recommendation quality and playlist cohesion — and those two things are what keep listeners coming back, which is the metric that actually drives revenue here.

The NLP task. I've formulated this as a multi-class text classification problem: given only a song's lyrics, predict its genre from a fixed set of labels. I deliberately picked lyrics over audio features for two reasons — they're cheap to pull from any licensing feed without needing a separate audio pipeline, and a lyrics-only model can sit alongside an existing audio-based classifier as a second opinion, flagging tracks where the two disagree for a human to look at.

Data collection. Lyrics and genre labels come from the Lyrics Genre Dataset (Large) on Hugging Face (Yegor25, 2025), which itself was pulled together from Genius.com song pages and covers millions of tracks across languages and genres. I filtered this down to English-language, single-genre-labelled tracks and balanced it to six mainstream genres — Pop, Rap, Rock, Hip-Hop, R&B, Country — at 1,500 songs each (9,000 total), using the curation script in pipeline/curate_lyrics.py. Balancing matters here because the raw pool skews roughly 37% Pop, and I didn't want the model to just learn "predict Pop" as a shortcut; it also keeps the notebook's runtime and character budget manageable.

2. High-Level System Design¶

I split the pipeline into six connected components. Each one hands a concrete artefact to the next:

# Component Role
1Data IngestionLoads the curated, balanced lyrics–genre CSV
2PreprocessingRegex cleaning + NLTK tokenisation, stop-word removal, lemmatisation
3Information ExtractionspaCy NER — auxiliary structured signal (entity density) per genre
4RepresentationTwo parallel branches: sparse TF-IDF vs dense Transformer embeddings (Hugging Face MiniLM)
5ModelingNaive Bayes & Logistic Regression trained per representation
6Evaluation & ReportingAccuracy / macro-F1 / confusion matrix; business recommendation

Why carry two representations instead of picking one? Because they fail differently. TF-IDF is sparse and fast, and you can point at exactly which word pushed a prediction one way — that's useful when someone asks "why did the model think this is rap?" Transformer embeddings are dense and pick up on semantic similarity that pure word-overlap misses, at the cost of being harder to inspect. Comparing the two head-to-head is really part of the evaluation, not just a design choice made up front.

4. Preprocessing (NLTK)¶

Raw Genius lyrics come with structural tags like [Chorus] and [Verse 1] baked in, plus inconsistent casing and punctuation — none of which tells you anything about genre, it's just noise. The cleaning step strips all of that out, then NLTK handles tokenisation, English stop-word removal, and WordNet lemmatisation so that inflected forms (dancing, danced → dance) don't get counted as separate features.

In [4]:
import nltk
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.stem import WordNetLemmatizer
from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize

stop_words = set(stopwords.words('english'))
lemmatizer = WordNetLemmatizer()

def clean_text(text):
    text = re.sub(r'\[.*?\]', ' ', text)          # drop [Chorus]/[Verse] tags
    text = re.sub(r'[^a-zA-Z\s]', ' ', text)       # keep letters only
    return re.sub(r'\s+', ' ', text).strip().lower()

def preprocess(text):
    tokens = word_tokenize(clean_text(text))
    return ' '.join(lemmatizer.lemmatize(t) for t in tokens if t not in stop_words and len(t) > 2)

df['clean'] = df['lyrics'].apply(clean_text)
df['processed'] = df['clean'].apply(preprocess)

5. Information Extraction (spaCy)¶

Before jumping into modelling, I wanted a second, simpler signal to sanity-check against. spaCy's pretrained NER tags named entities — people, organisations, places — in each song, and my hunch was that genres name-drop at very different rates. If that holds up, it's weak evidence of genre before you've even looked at the rest of the language, and it's the kind of descriptive, information-extraction insight that's useful on its own rather than only as a model feature.

In [5]:
import spacy
nlp = spacy.load('en_core_web_sm', disable=['tagger','parser','lemmatizer','attribute_ruler'])

sample = df.groupby('genre', group_keys=False).apply(lambda g: g.sample(150, random_state=42))
person_density = Counter()
for doc, genre in zip(nlp.pipe(sample['lyrics'].str.slice(0, 1200), batch_size=50), sample['genre']):
    person_density[genre] += sum(e.label_ == 'PERSON' for e in doc.ents)

density = pd.Series({g: person_density[g] / 150 for g in df['genre'].unique()}).sort_values()

6. Text Representation¶

Branch A — TF-IDF. Unigrams and bigrams, capped at the top 8,000 features by document frequency (min_df=3), which down-weights words that show up everywhere regardless of genre.

Branch B — Transformer embeddings. sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 from Hugging Face — a distilled BERT-family model — turns each song into a 384-dimensional, mean-pooled contextual embedding. The idea is that this should pick up on semantic similarity (synonyms, paraphrased ideas) that plain word-overlap just can't see.

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModel

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained('sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained('sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2').eval()

def embed(texts, batch_size=64):
    out_all = []
    with torch.no_grad():
        for i in range(0, len(texts), batch_size):
            batch = texts[i:i+batch_size]
            enc = tok(batch, padding=True, truncation=True, max_length=256, return_tensors='pt')
            out = model(**enc)
            mask = enc['attention_mask'].unsqueeze(-1).float()
            pooled = (out.last_hidden_state * mask).sum(1) / mask.sum(1)
            out_all.append(pooled.numpy())
    return np.vstack(out_all)

t0 = time.time()
X_embed = embed(df['clean'].tolist())
print('Embedded', X_embed.shape, 'in', round(time.time()-t0,1), 's')

Xe_train, Xe_test = train_test_split(X_embed, test_size=0.2, stratify=df['genre'], random_state=42)[0:2]

7. Modeling¶

I trained three classifiers: Multinomial Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression on TF-IDF as fast, interpretable baselines, and Logistic Regression again on the MiniLM embeddings. Keeping the classifier fixed for that last one and only swapping the representation is deliberate — it isolates whether the embedding itself is doing anything extra, rather than mixing two variables at once.

In [8]:
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, f1_score, classification_report, confusion_matrix

nb = MultinomialNB().fit(X_train_tfidf, y_train)
lr_tfidf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=2).fit(X_train_tfidf, y_train)
lr_embed = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=2).fit(Xe_train, y_train)

results = {}
for name, (clf, Xte) in {
    'NaiveBayes + TF-IDF': (nb, X_test_tfidf),
    'LogReg + TF-IDF': (lr_tfidf, X_test_tfidf),
    'LogReg + MiniLM': (lr_embed, Xe_test),
}.items():
    pred = clf.predict(Xte)
    results[name] = {'accuracy': accuracy_score(y_test, pred), 'macro_f1': f1_score(y_test, pred, average='macro'), 'pred': pred}

pd.DataFrame(results).T[['accuracy','macro_f1']].round(3)

I'm reporting both accuracy and macro-F1 for all three combinations — macro-F1 matters here because it's unweighted across genres, so a model can't just coast by nailing the easy classes. 9. Final Discussion¶

Findings. All three models sit in the 0.40–0.43 accuracy range, well above the 0.167 you'd get from guessing randomly across six balanced classes. LogReg on the MiniLM embeddings came out on top for macro-F1, ahead of both TF-IDF baselines, which does support the idea that contextual embeddings pick up genre-relevant signal that raw word-overlap misses — though honestly the margin over TF-IDF isn't huge, so I wouldn't oversell it. The confusion matrix tells a clearer story: Rap and Country separate cleanly, each with its own distinctive, self-referential vocabulary, while Pop and Hip-Hop get confused with each other and with Rap constantly. That lines up with what you'd expect given how much pop-rap crossover exists in practice, and probably also reflects genre labels at the source that were never that consistent to begin with. Separately, the spaCy NER pass backs this up from a different angle — Rap and Hip-Hop songs name-drop roughly 2–3× more people per song than Rock or Country, which the classifier isn't even using directly.

Strengths. Everything here is reproducible from a cited, versioned dataset rather than a black box. I compared three model/representation combinations instead of just reporting one number, and I kept the NER signal as a separate descriptive check rather than folding it straight into the model — that way the evaluation stays honest about what's actually driving the predictions versus what's just a suggestive side-finding.

Limitations. A few things I'd flag before anyone takes this too far. First, the genre labels are self-reported/community-tagged at the source and were never independently verified, so some of what looks like "model confusion" might just be inconsistent ground truth. Second, this only looks at lyrics — audio is normally the stronger genre signal, so I'd see this as a complement to an audio-based system, not a replacement for one. Third, the NER entity-density numbers came from a 150-song-per-genre sample for the sake of runtime, so they're a useful descriptive pointer rather than something I've statistically tested.

Business implications & recommendation. At around 40% accuracy, this isn't reliable enough to auto-tag a catalog on its own — but it's more than good enough to act as a cheap disagreement detector sitting alongside whatever audio-based tagger the platform already runs. Wherever the two disagree, especially around the Pop/Hip-Hop/Rap boundary, route that track to a human reviewer instead of reviewing the whole catalog blind. That concentrates manual effort where it's actually needed, and the name-drop density from the NER step could be folded in later as a cheap extra feature if it's worth squeezing out more accuracy.
